Global Thermonuclear War

is my most well-developed solo game, in terms of completeness, fluidity in style, and mechanic depth. In the first year after release, it got over 2,000 plays and 5,000 views on itch.io.

The game is a mostly-faithful recreation of the WOPR Global Thermonuclear War simulator from the movie War Games. Gameplay is fully text-based, featuring an ASCII-art GUI, animated text, and a companion manual to assist with gameplay. The game adjusts its difficulty (intelligence and response times) based on the players' skill, allowing for a naturally integrated difficulty scaling, always attempting to match the player's aggression in hopes of a no-winners scenario.

I developed the game in a month, working solo, using the Phaser3 Phaser.js framework. My goal when creating this game was to cultivate an immersive experience that didn't operate off of knee-jerk skills or reaction times; I found the development experience much more enjoyable when it didn't require me to worry about a physics system, hitbox collisions, or something similar; I was able to fully invest my time into pushing for immersion, both in feelings of command-based, unusual gameplay and UI design.

As probably expected, my largest challenge with this project was "student difficulty"; due to the game's non-traditional nature, a lot of my time was spent refining mechanics, language, and playability, so the game was comprehensible to someone who picked it up and spent ten minutes playing.

Slash & Burn

was a collaborative project with a team of four for UCSC's Independent Gamedev Coalition club.

Over the course of a quarter (ten weeks) we developed the game using Godot. We showcased the game at the Art for Games & Playable Media department-sponsored end-of-year games showcase under our club's banner!

Slash & Burn is an arcade style play-till-you-lose "arena defense" type game where the player defends a central campfire from hordes of enemies spawning at the edges of a forest grove. The game's core mechanic is that each type of enemy can only be defeated by one type of weapon, and the player can only hold and throw one weapon at a time, leading to high-focus scenarios in which the player must prioritize weapon placement and ideal pathing.

My role in the project was fairly straightforwards; I headed UI design, enemy spawning & pathing, wave management, and the firepit. Some unique challenges I was presented with during this project were the limitations and confusions of working within Godot; my team decided to work with Godot to broaden our horizons, since we'd never used it as a tool before. Additionally, it was the first time I'd seriously published to itch.io, so a lot of care was taken into making sure the game was suitable for web-browser play.



Automanora

was the final group project (4-week development timeline) for my Fall 2024 class CMPM 125 - Game Technologies, a class that focused on the low and mid-complexity features of Unity. Automanora was voted as the "best aesthetic" game during the end-of-quarter game showcase for CMPM 125.
My role during this project was one of the mechanic design leads, as well as the graphic designer. It was my first time using Unity's new UI Builder system, and I really enjoyed working with Builder over the older UI system.

My favorite part of this game, though, was creating the 'living' title screen. I wrote the music for the title screen, designed the game's logo, and had a lot of fun building the cityscape from out-of-box assets and animating the camera path.

As a reflection on the project as a whole, I was slightly less than satisfied with it. While we managed to get a lot of the content we originally imagined into our game -- including sound effects, intro cutscenes, multiple levels, and several puzzle mechanisms -- we also wanted to include more of a story, which may have came in the form of voice lines, notes being found for the player to read, or environmental/visual storytelling methods. However, due to our lack of assets and time, this was cut. We also could have focused on making a couple great levels rather than 4 good levels, as well as spent a little more time bug-fixing, because we were working right up to the deadline adding content rather than debugging.

121Farm

was the final group project for my Fall 2024 class CMPM 121 - Game Development Patterns, a class centered around building professional-grade codebases and readable, efficient code.

Drawing from Martin Fowler's Refactoring Improving the Design of Existing Code, this project was focused less on playability or aesthetics, and was all about the code itself. 121Farm was my first experience working with localization, persistent saving over web-based gameplay sessions, and formatting a game to be played/downloaded as a Progressive Web App.

My role for this project was all over the place, but my major contributions consisted of:

  • Decoupling byte data from our "board" class and converting it from a 2d typescript array of objects into a 1d ArrayBuffer.
  • Designing a simple External DSL that controlled the generation parameters of the game board, as well as define time-based events.
  • Setting up our project to work with Vite and the VitePWA plugin, which allowed us to build our webpage and publish it as a progressive web app.
  • Writing development logs for our team as the project progressed.
